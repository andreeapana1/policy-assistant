Rules-based search answered instantly (about 2 ms) and uses no tokens, but because it only matches keywords and a hand-written synonym list it identified the correct policy for just 68% of the 22 test questions. It failed whenever an employee's wording differed from the policy's (“a journalist called” matched the Social Media Policy instead of the Media Contact Policy), and because it always returns its best keyword match it presented unrelated policies as answers, such as the Office Access (badge) policy for “Can I bring my dog to the office?”; 6 of its 22 answers (27%) were unsupported by the policy database and it declined only 2 of the 4 questions that no policy covers. The LLM without a vector index, which receives all 98 policies in every prompt, identified the correct policy in 100% of cases in about 1.1 s, but used about 2,970 tokens per question, a cost that grows with every policy added. The LLM with a vector index retrieves the three most similar policies and sends only those, using about 330 tokens per question (9× fewer) at about 1.3 s (slightly slower because of the extra embedding call) and identified the correct policy in 95% of cases; its one miss was “Are jeans allowed on Fridays?”, where it declined instead of citing the Dress Code Policy.

Both LLM approaches were far less likely than rules-based search to give answers the database does not support: each had one flagged answer (5%), both correctly declined all four questions with no matching policy, and on partly covered questions (such as the number of sick days per year) they reported what the policy says and noted that it gives no number instead of inventing one. The flagged answer of the LLM without an index was a genuine fabrication (it said only authorised employees may speak to “waterfowl or media”), while the vector approach’s flag was a cautious remark that the Work From Abroad Policy does not name specific countries, which the automatic judge rated as unsupported. My preferred approach is the LLM with a vector index: it matches the full-context LLM on grounding, comes close on accuracy and response time, and uses about a ninth of the tokens, and because retrieval keeps the prompt small however many policies exist, it scales to a real policy library that would not fit in one prompt, whereas the full-context approach becomes slower and more expensive as the database grows. It is therefore the approach used in the Slack bot, which shows the cited policy text next to every answer so employees can verify it.
